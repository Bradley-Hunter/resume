A few months ago I wrote about using AI to turn half-formed project ideas into real design documents. That process worked well enough that I ended up with 16 design docs for a metroidvania I'm building called Ashes at the Crossroads, covering everything from the combat system to the world structure to the economy. The documents were detailed, and I felt good about them. The problem was that I had no way to know if they were actually good.

I'm a software developer, not a game designer. I can write a document describing how a parry-based combat system should work, and it will read well and sound reasonable, but I don't have the experience to evaluate whether the timing values I chose will feel right to a player who has spent thousands of hours in the genre. I don't have the narrative design background to know if a story beat will land or fall flat in a metroidvania format. And I definitely can't see the places where two of my systems interact in ways that break both of them, because I designed both systems and my own assumptions are baked into each one.

AI has the domain knowledge to fill those gaps. I knew this because the planning process I wrote about in my previous post relied on AI's breadth of knowledge to help me think through design decisions I wouldn't have been able to research on my own. But when I tried asking AI to review my finished design documents, the feedback I got back was surface-level and agreeable. It would tell me what I wanted to hear, point out a few minor issues, and broadly affirm that the design was solid. It wasn't pushing back hard enough, and it wasn't catching the deeper problems that come from systems interacting in ways I hadn't considered. I needed a way to make AI disagree with itself, because the gaps in my design weren't going to be found by a single perspective telling me everything looked fine.

## Building the First Personas

The idea that led to a solution was something I already knew about AI: giving it a persona helps guide its output in a specific direction. If you tell AI to respond as a combat designer with strong opinions about game feel, it produces different and more focused feedback than if you just ask it to review a combat system. This is what got me started, but what I ended up building went significantly further than a simple persona prompt.

What I built were structured character definitions. Each persona has a name, a detailed background that establishes their expertise, a specific domain they evaluate, and, most importantly, an explicit set of blind spots. The blind spots are not accidental limitations. They are designed into each persona on purpose, because they are what create productive disagreement.

The first set had five game design personas, and each one reads the same design document through a completely different lens. Kira is the genre veteran with over 2000 hours across metroidvanias. She evaluates player experience, pacing, and whether mechanics create real decisions or busywork. Her blind spot is that she doesn't think about implementation cost at all, and she will ask for things that are expensive to build without caring. Marcus is the narrative designer, mythology-obsessed, who evaluates thematic resonance and whether the story earns its emotional beats. His blind spot is that he will prioritize narrative integrity over gameplay flow. Dev is the scope realist, a solo indie developer who shipped a small game through early access. He evaluates MVP feasibility and hidden implementation costs. His blind spot is that he will cut things that are genuinely important if they look expensive. Ryn is the systems designer who thinks in feedback loops and evaluates where dominant strategies collapse the design. Her blind spot is that she over-engineers for depth. And Ash is the technical architect who evaluates whether designs map cleanly to the game's software architecture. His blind spot is that he will reject good designs because they're awkward to implement.

The reason the blind spots matter so much is that they create friction that mirrors real disagreements between real experts. When Kira wants an expensive feature because it would make the game better for players, and Dev wants to cut that same feature because a solo developer can't afford to build it, the tension between them surfaces a real tradeoff that I then have to resolve. A genre veteran and a scope realist genuinely do optimize for different things, and neither of them is wrong. That friction is the entire point of the system, because it forces problems into the open that a single agreeable reviewer would never surface.

## Putting Them to Work

Having personas that disagree is only useful if there's a process that channels the disagreement into actual decisions rather than noise. So alongside the personas, I developed a structured review workflow.

A review session starts by loading the persona definitions and the design documents into the AI's context. I specify which documents are the ones being reviewed and which are reference context, meaning they're there so the personas can check for contradictions with previous decisions but they aren't being re-reviewed themselves. Each persona reads the primary documents through their own lens and surfaces concerns, each one tagged with a severity rating: blocks shipping, degrades quality, or worth discussing. The personas also call out what the document does well, because knowing what's working is as important as knowing what isn't.

After the individual reads, the personas discuss among themselves. They debate severity, refine their positions, push back on each other, and surface new problems that emerge from the friction between their different perspectives. From there, problems get presented to me one at a time, with the personas laying out what's at stake, which of them are on which side, and what options they see. I respond with my own perspective and context, they react in character, and we go back and forth until the problem is resolved or I make a final call.

Every decision in this process is mine. This is the core rule of the entire system and the one I enforce most strictly because it's the one the AI is most inclined to break. The personas propose, argue, and push back, but they do not get to decide. When the AI has resolved a disagreement on its own without my input, I've corrected it immediately, because the moment you let AI make decisions by consensus you get the same problem I started with: agreeable, middle-ground answers that avoid conflict. The value of the system depends entirely on the human being the one who makes every call.

One thing I discovered early on is that the first version of this process included a built-in moderator persona whose job was to keep discussions organized and prevent any single persona from dominating. It kept things tidy, but it also killed the best conversations. The moderator would step in right when things got heated between personas, right when Kira and Dev were genuinely arguing about whether a feature was worth its cost, and it would summarize both positions and move the discussion along before the argument reached its most productive point. I found that when I moderated sessions myself and let the personas argue without a referee cooling things down, they pushed harder on their positions, brought up edge cases to support their side, and made concessions that felt earned rather than polite. The conversations used more tokens, but the quality of the insights was significantly better. The best decisions I've gotten from this system came from debates that the built-in moderator would have cut short.

## The First Real Tests

The first review session I ran targeted the combat system, healing mechanics, and the two boss fights in the first biome. I loaded the five persona definitions, fed in three design documents, and let them work. Six decisions came out of that session, each one a concrete change to the design. The combat designer flagged that the first boss had a redundant attack in his kit that competed with the actual climax of the fight, so the attack got cut. The narrative designer caught that a post-boss story scroll contained six revelations crammed into one readable item, which contradicted the game's own principle of discovering story through environment rather than text dumps. The solution split the scroll into personal content that stayed on the scroll and broader story details that moved into environmental objects in the boss's camp. The session also flagged five items it couldn't resolve because they depended on systems that hadn't been designed yet, like form switching mechanics and the stamina system. Those became the known unknowns that drove the next round of reviews.

That first session proved the concept well enough that I kept going. Over the next day I ran seven more review sessions covering every remaining design document in the project: death and save systems, economy, narrative and tone, powers and abilities, world structure, modding, and the sigil system. Each review built on the ones before it because the decisions from earlier sessions became reference context for later ones. By the end of that push, roughly 40 decisions were locked down and about 20 items were flagged for later resolution. The design documents went from something I thought was solid to something that had been stress-tested from five expert perspectives across every system in the game.

## The Review That Changed the Most

A few days after the single-system reviews, I decided to run a cross-disciplinary session where both persona sets, the five game design personas and the five architecture personas I had built for a separate set of technical reviews, would read every document at the same time. The goal was to find problems that only become visible when you look at the whole picture rather than individual systems.

Two personas flagged the same problem independently, which is what told me it was real. The game is called a shapeshifting metroidvania. Shapeshifting is the core identity in every piece of marketing and design language. The entire first biome, which is the only content in the MVP and the only thing players would experience in the early access release, had no shapeshifting at all. A player who bought the game based on the shapeshifting pitch would finish the first release having never transformed. The combat designer and the player advocate both caught this. The narrative designer pushed back, arguing that the shapeshifting unlock was designed as a mid-game milestone and moving it into the first biome would cheapen the moment. Both sides were right, which is exactly the kind of problem that only surfaces when experts from different disciplines look at the same design.

I spent three days trying to fix the problem. I designed a 22-room restructured biome that included wolf form combat, a new boss, a complete wolf moveset with a three-hit attack chain and a dash-to-sprint movement system, and a form-switching input model with a tap toggle and a hold-for-radial-wheel selector. The first iteration crammed roughly 18 to 20 new mechanics into a space that originally taught 10 to 12, which crushed the pacing. Each subsequent iteration cut more. The third and fourth iterations took about 20 minutes each before it became clear that the direction itself was wrong, not just the scope.

I reverted to the original first biome design. The shapeshifting game still doesn't have shapeshifting in the MVP.

But those three days of work produced the combo system, the form-switching input model, the complete wolf moveset, and the decision to replace the entire stamina resource system with per-form cooldowns. Every one of those decisions applies regardless of which biome wolf form eventually appears in. The restructure itself failed, but the pressure it created forced system-level decisions into existence that wouldn't have happened otherwise.

That cross-disciplinary session fed directly into a mechanics refinement session that resolved 13 concrete timing values in a single sitting. To give a sense of how these values were derived, let me walk through one chain. The parry window, which is the amount of time the player has to perfectly time a defensive move, is 0.133 seconds. The stagger duration, which is how long an enemy is vulnerable after being parried, is 0.9 seconds. The base form's attack speed is 0.35 seconds per swing, which means a two-hit attack chain takes 0.7 seconds plus about 0.1 seconds for the player to position themselves, totaling 0.8 seconds. A heal takes 1.0 seconds. At a stagger duration of 0.9 seconds, the player can fit two attacks into the stagger window but cannot fit a heal. That constraint is deliberate, because it means the stagger window forces the player to choose between dealing damage and recovering health. The number isn't arbitrary. It's the exact value that makes the stagger window a meaningful decision rather than a free reward. Each of the 13 values was derived this way, flowing mathematically from the ones resolved before it, with the personas arguing through the implications of each number while I made the final calls.

## Finding Bugs in Code That Doesn't Exist

After the design reviews settled what the game should be, I turned the same approach on the software architecture. The game uses a somewhat unusual technical setup where the game engine Godot handles everything visual, a Rust ECS (Entity Component System, a way of organizing game logic through composable parts) handles all game logic, and a bridge layer connects the two by passing messages back and forth. I had written six layers of architecture documents describing how every system would be built.

I ran a systematic cross-reference of every architecture document against every design document, checking whether every claim in the architecture ("this system listens for this event") had a matching claim in the design ("this event gets emitted when this happens"), and vice versa. The cross-reference found 19 places where the two sets of documents disagreed.

Four of them were critical, meaning they would have been "why doesn't this work" moments the first time I tried to implement the feature. The combat system relied on per-frame animation events to spawn hitboxes during attacks, but the animation system only had an event for when an animation finished. No per-frame events meant no hitboxes, which meant no attacks would work at all. The pogo mechanic, where the player bounces off an enemy by attacking downward, had a hit event defined but no system anywhere to apply the upward bounce. The player would pogo, the hit would register, damage would apply, and then nothing would happen because nobody had built the bounce. Contact damage, which is just the basic concept of taking damage when you walk into an enemy, had no collision detection or hitbox system at all. And a single ambiguous sentence in a design document had led to two different systems being assigned to the same controller button, which would have caused the player to accidentally heal when trying to recover lost currency.

All 19 gaps were found and resolved before a single line of game code was written. Finding them in documents cost me one focused session. Finding them in code would have cost hours of debugging per gap, with the added frustration of code that should work but doesn't because the architecture assumed something that didn't exist.

After seeing what the gap analysis caught, I expanded the persona system further and built a set of five security-focused personas, each specializing in a different aspect of data integrity. Rhea analyzes trust boundaries, Leander focuses on achievement integrity, Nadia handles mod sandboxing, Eira specializes in save file safety, and Jasper evaluates defensive coding practices. They reviewed every design and architecture document together and found 22 issues. Three were flagged as must-fix before implementation: the save system had no atomic write protection, meaning a power failure during a save could corrupt the file permanently. There was no error handling policy distinguishing between a base game data failure (which is a development bug and should crash) and a mod data failure (which should skip the mod and notify the player). And there was no inventory of every point where external data enters the system, which meant some trust boundaries had validation and others didn't. All 22 issues were resolved in the design documents before implementation began.

## Why This Works

At this point it's fair to ask whether this is just asking AI to roleplay as experts. The reason I think it's something more than that comes down to three specific things that generic roleplay prompts don't have.

The first is that the personas have designed limitations. When you ask AI to "pretend to be a combat designer," it produces an AI that tries to be right about everything. Kira specifically doesn't care about implementation cost. Dev specifically undervalues long-term depth. These blind spots are what create the productive friction, because the personas end up optimizing for different things and disagreeing in predictable, useful ways. A single AI trying to be a general expert doesn't produce this kind of friction.

The second is that the process is structured. There's a defined workflow for how concerns get surfaced, how they're prioritized by severity, how they're debated in dependency order, and how they're resolved one at a time. The structure is what prevents the conversations from wandering into vague territory and ensures that every problem gets a concrete resolution.

The third, and the one I consider most important, is that every decision is human. The AI argues, but it does not decide. This is the rule I enforce most strictly because it's the one that prevents the system from collapsing into the same agreeable mush that made me build it in the first place.

## Where It Stands Now

What started as five game design personas built to evaluate a combat system has grown into 13 complete persona sets with over 100 personas spanning four phases of the project. None of this growth was planned. The first set existed because I needed help evaluating combat design. The second set of five architecture personas existed because I needed help translating game designs into software patterns. Each set after that was built because a specific gap appeared in the process that the existing personas couldn't cover.

The design phase alone now has 11 sets covering game design, architecture, cross-discipline review, business strategy, level design, narrative and writing, player archetypes, accessibility, art direction, audio design, and document integration. The player archetypes set is particularly different from the others because it contains nine simulated players rather than experts. There's a completionist, a speedrunner, a lore hunter, a combat optimizer, a casual explorer, a genre first-timer, and others. They stress-test finished designs from the receiving end, reacting emotionally to design decisions the way a real player would, which surfaces problems that expert analysis misses.

The implementation phase has a single set of 26 personas that work differently from the design sets. Instead of actively debating, they stay loaded during coding sessions and remain silent unless the current task touches their domain or they disagree with something. They're passive advisors rather than a panel. During implementation, I don't need five people arguing about architecture. I need a bridge specialist who speaks up when I'm about to route something through the wrong layer.

Four review sets evaluate completed work after the fact, covering code review, testing and QA, performance optimization, and security and integrity. Two cross-phase sets span the whole project lifecycle for localization readiness and post-launch operations.

The system costs real tokens and real time. Cross-disciplinary reviews eat through context quickly, and the mechanics refinement session that derived 13 timing values was a long and expensive conversation. I consider it a good trade. The cost of finding a fundamental design flaw in documents is measured in tokens. The cost of finding it in code is measured in weeks of rework. The 19 architectural gaps that the gap analysis caught would have cost me more debugging time than every review session I've run combined.

As of this writing I still don't have a line of game code written. That's the point. When I do start writing code, the design will have been examined from over 100 different expert perspectives, with every disagreement resolved and every decision documented. The gaps that remain will be the ones I chose to defer, not the ones I failed to see.
