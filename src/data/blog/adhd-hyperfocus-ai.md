When hyperfocus hits, I'm not just coding. I'm inside the problem. The rest of the world mutes itself, and I stop being aware that I'm hungry, that I'm tired, that my back has been screaming at me for the last two hours. There's only the next line and the next fix and the next feature. For me, that state is where my best work happens, but it's also incredibly fragile.

## The AI Promise

AI coding tools are supposed to make you faster, and for an ADHD brain, faster sounds like the dream. Less friction between the idea and the implementation, less time fighting boilerplate, less time looking up syntax I've seen a hundred times. In theory, it keeps me in the flow longer because there's less busywork pulling me out of it.

For a while I believed that, and I still mostly do. But I've run into a problem I didn't anticipate, and it's one that comes directly from the thing that makes AI tools useful in the first place.

## The Review Problem

When AI writes a block of code, it's not my code yet. I didn't think through every line, which means I have to stop and actually read it, understand it, and make sure it does what I think it does. That sounds reasonable, and it is reasonable. But for me, that stop is a trapdoor.

Hyperfocus works by narrowing my attention to a single thread. I'm not just thinking about the current function. I'm holding the next three things I need to do in my head, the reason I'm doing them, and the context behind the context. When I stop to read AI-generated code, I'm not just pausing. I'm asking my brain to switch from build mode into review mode, and those two modes don't coexist well for me. One of them wins, and it's usually not the one I need.

## What Actually Happens

This is where the AI promise collides with how my brain actually works. Usually one of two things happens when I break focus to review AI output.

The more common one is a sudden awareness of my body. All at once I notice that I'm hungry, that I need to use the bathroom, that my neck is stiff, that my eyes are dry. These signals were there the whole time, but I couldn't perceive them while I was deep in flow. The moment hyperfocus breaks, they all arrive at once.

The other outcome, not far behind, is a boredom cascade. I lose the thread of what I was building, why it mattered, and what came next. Once that thread is gone, so is the pull. The momentum disappears. I close the editor and end up doing something else entirely, one of my hobbies, something low-stakes that my brain can engage with without the context it just lost.

Neither of these are a great outcome when I was in the middle of building something.

## What Helps (A Little)

I want to be clear that these are things that work for me and I'm not offering a protocol.

The most reliable thing I've found is physically writing down what I was about to do next before I stop to review anything. A sticky note with one line: what is the next thing I need to implement after this. Not a digital note, not a comment in the code, but actual pen on paper. Something that exists in my physical space and doesn't disappear when I switch windows. When review breaks my flow, that note is a handhold I can grab to pull myself back into where I was.

The other thing is intentional breaks. Before I go too deep, before I get three hours in and fully unreachable, stopping deliberately, checking in, taking a breath. This one is harder, because by definition the deeper I am in hyperfocus the less aware I am that I should take a break. The state that makes breaks most useful is the same state that makes me least likely to remember to take one. I don't have a clean solution for that, and I'm still working on it.

What I do know is that AI tools are here to stay, and for neurodivergent programmers they come with tradeoffs that don't get talked about much. The speed is real, and the review cost is also real. For my brain specifically, understanding that tension has been more useful than pretending it doesn't exist.
